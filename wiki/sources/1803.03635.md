## Problem

Pruning techniques can reduce network size by over 90% without harming accuracy, yet the sparse architectures they produce are difficult to train from scratch — reaching lower accuracy than the original network when trained from a random initialization. This raises the question: if the pruned network is smaller, why can't we simply train it instead of the large network? The paper resolves this paradox.

## Method

**The Lottery Ticket Hypothesis**: A randomly-initialized dense network contains subnetworks ("winning tickets") that, when trained in isolation *from their original initialization*, can match the test accuracy of the full network in at most the same number of iterations — at ≤10-20% of the parameter count.

**Algorithm — Iterative Magnitude Pruning (IMP) with weight rewinding:**

1. Randomly initialize f(x; θ₀) where θ₀ ∼ D_θ
2. Train for j iterations → θⱼ
3. Prune p% of parameters in θⱼ by smallest magnitude → binary mask m ∈ {0,1}^|θ|
4. **Reset** surviving parameters to their original initialization: winning ticket = f(x; m⊙θ₀)
5. Repeat for n rounds, pruning p^(1/n)% each round (iterative > one-shot)

**The key innovation is step 4** — resetting to θ₀, not reinitializing randomly. Random reinitialization of the surviving weights destroys performance, proving that the initialization values themselves are load-bearing, not just the sparsity structure.

Formally, winning ticket f(x; m⊙θ₀) satisfies:
- j' ≤ j (trains in commensurate iterations)
- a' ≥ a (reaches commensurate accuracy)  
- ‖m‖₀ ≪ |θ| (far fewer parameters)

**Lottery Ticket Conjecture** (extension): SGD actively seeks out and trains a subset of well-initialized weights. Dense networks are easier to train because they provide more candidate subnetworks from which training might recover a winning ticket.

## Results

**MNIST (Lenet):**
- Winning tickets at ≤21% of original parameter count
- Above that size, winning tickets learn faster and reach higher accuracy than the original network
- Randomly reinitialized winning tickets perform far worse — structure alone does not explain success

**CIFAR10 (Conv-2, Conv-4, Conv-6):**
- Winning tickets at ≤10% of original parameter count
- Consistent across SGD, momentum, and Adam optimizers
- Robust to dropout, weight decay, batchnorm, and residual connections

**Learning rate sensitivity**: Deeper networks require learning rate warmup to find winning tickets at higher learning rates — a practical constraint not present in the smaller architectures.

**Iterative vs. one-shot pruning**: Iterative IMP finds winning tickets at smaller sizes than one-shot pruning at the same accuracy level.

## Significance

The Lottery Ticket Hypothesis is the theoretical cornerstone of training-time pruning. Three implications for the thesis:

1. **Why IMP works**: This paper explains the mechanistic reason Han et al.'s iterative magnitude pruning [arxiv:1506.02626] is effective — it uncovers winning tickets whose initial weights were already configured for learning. The reset step is what distinguishes IMP from standard post-training pruning.

2. **Initialization lottery for shared weights (HaLViT implication)**: HaLViT's weight sharing constrains multiple layers to use the same parameter θ₀. If a winning ticket exists in the dense HaLViT network, its sparsity mask m must be compatible with the W+Wᵀ dual-use: zeroing (i,j) simultaneously zeroes (j,i) in the transposed pathway. The "initialization lottery" for a shared weight is therefore entangled — the single θ₀ must serve as a good initialization for all layers sharing it. Whether shared weights have a better or worse chance of winning the lottery than unshared weights is an open empirical question.

3. **Contrast with SparseGPT** [arxiv:2301.00774]: SparseGPT is a one-shot PTQ-style pruner that avoids any retraining. LTH requires retraining from the original initialization. For LLMs where retraining is prohibitive, SparseGPT is the practical choice. For smaller models like HaLViT on Vision tasks, LTH-style iterative pruning is feasible and would find sparser, more accurate subnetworks.

## Contradicts / Supports

- **Supports** 1506.02626 (Han et al.): iterative magnitude pruning is the algorithm both papers use; LTH provides the theoretical explanation for why it works
- **Contrasts** [[sources/2301.00774]] (SparseGPT): SparseGPT is no-retrain one-shot; LTH requires iterative retraining from original init — complementary (SparseGPT scales to LLMs; LTH gives higher accuracy where retraining is feasible)
- **Supports** lecun1990-obd (OBD): both use saliency to find the right weights to keep; OBD uses second-order Hessian saliency, LTH uses magnitude + the rewind insight
